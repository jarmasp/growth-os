# Linguistic Markers of Processing (Pennebaker / LIWC)

> How someone writes about an experience — specific word choices, not just
> content — carries a measurable signal of whether they've actually processed
> it or are just describing it.

## Core Idea

James Pennebaker's decades of research on expressive writing (originally
studying how people write about trauma and stressful events) found that the
*language* people use when writing about an experience predicts psychological
and even physical outcomes, independent of what the experience was. He and
collaborators built LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count), a tool that
counts word categories in a text — causal words, cognitive-process words,
emotion words, pronouns — and correlates their frequency with health and
adjustment outcomes over time.

Two categories matter most for judging whether someone has processed an
experience rather than just reported it: **causal connectors** (because, since,
led to, caused, therefore, as a result) signal that the writer is constructing
an explanation, not just a timeline. **Insight words** (realize, understand, I
see that, I didn't know that, now I know) signal a moment of genuine cognitive
change — the writer noticing they know something they didn't before, not
restating what they already believed.

The finding that matters practically: people who increase their use of causal
and insight words across repeated writing about the same event show better
processing and adjustment than people whose word patterns stay flat — the
marker isn't just correlated with having processed something, writing in that
way appears to *help* process it.

## How Growth OS Applies It

`agents/reflection-scorer.md`'s **D5 — Linguistic Quality** (0–1) scans a
reflection's answers for exactly these two markers: a causal connector where
the subject is technical or structural (not an effort attribution — "because I
wasn't careful" doesn't count), and an insight marker paired with a specific
proposition. The rubric scores 1 only when *both* are present, treating their
absence as a signal that the reflection stayed at description rather than
reaching processing — independent of how long or detailed the answer is.
