# Ikigai

> A reason for being that sits at the intersection of what you love, what
> you're good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for — used
> here as a lens for finding where a stated goal and a stated motivation
> genuinely overlap, or don't.

## Core Idea

*Ikigai* (生き甲斐) is a Japanese concept, often translated loosely as "reason
for being" or "that which makes life worth living." It was studied
academically by psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya, whose 1966 work on the subject grew
out of her clinical work with patients recovering from serious illness and
examined what gave people's lives a sense of meaning and direction, independent
of external success. The now-widely-circulated four-circle Venn diagram
(passion = love + good at; mission = love + world needs; vocation = good at +
paid for; profession = paid for + world needs; ikigai = all four) is a later,
Western popularization rather than the original academic concept, which was
broader and less formulaic — but the four-axis version is useful precisely
because it's concrete enough to check someone's situation against.

The practical value of the four-axis lens isn't that everyone must find a
perfect overlap of all four axes to be fine — most real careers sit at a
partial overlap, and that's normal. Its value is diagnostic: naming *which*
axes are missing explains specific, nameable kinds of dissatisfaction. Good at
it and paid for it, but don't love it and it serves no purpose you care about
("comfortable but empty"). Love it and good at it, but can't get paid for it
("a hobby, not a living, yet"). The four axes turn a vague "something feels
off about my work" into a specific, addressable gap.

## How Growth OS Applies It

`agents/onboarding-agent.md`'s Phase 5 ("¿Por qué este oficio?") is framed as
the Ikigai intersection directly — explicitly looking for signals across love
(mastery drive, fascination with the craft), skill (implicit in the person's
own account of what they're good at), what the work serves (impact/relatedness
drive), and livelihood (the purely instrumental case, when none of the other
three show up). The phase's explicit framing — "this phase has no wrong
answers, the goal is clarity, not judgment" — matches Ikigai's own spirit: it's
a lens for noticing where the axes do or don't overlap, not a verdict on
whether someone's current work is good enough.
