# Immunity to Change

> People rarely fail to change their stated goals out of weak will — they fail
> because a hidden, equally real *competing commitment* is quietly working
> against the stated goal, protecting them from a fear they haven't
> named, and that competing commitment usually wins.

## Core Idea

Kegan and Lahey's framework starts from an observation: smart, motivated
people routinely fail to achieve goals they genuinely want, not from lack of
effort but because they're unknowingly running an "immune system" against
their own change — a set of self-protective behaviors defending against a
hidden fear, in service of a *competing commitment* that's just as real as the
stated goal, just unexamined. Their diagnostic method maps four columns: the
stated improvement goal; the behaviors that work *against* that goal (which
look like simple bad habits but are actually doing protective work); the
hidden competing commitment those behaviors serve ("I'm committed to never
appearing incompetent in front of my team," underneath "I procrastinate on
asking for help"); and the "big assumption" underneath that commitment — an
unexamined belief about what would happen if the protective behavior stopped
("if I ask for help, they'll see I don't belong here").

The framework's core move is *naming* the competing commitment explicitly,
without judgment — not as a character flaw, but as a real commitment doing
real (if ultimately counterproductive) psychological work. Naming it reduces
its hidden power, because a commitment operating silently in the background
can't be examined, tested, or weighed against the stated goal; once it's on
the table next to the stated goal, the person can actually decide whether the
trade-off it's making is one they'd choose on purpose.

## How Growth OS Applies It

`agents/onboarding-agent.md` uses this framework across two phases. Phase 2
("¿A dónde vas?") listens for a goal paired with a competing obligation and
explicitly surfaces the possible conflict: "Si chocaran — si avanzar hacia
{goal} requiriera sacrificar {obligation} — ¿cuál ganaría?" Phase 3 ("¿Qué está
en el camino?") listens for a blocker that's actually a competing commitment in
disguise and names it directly, without casting it as a flaw: "Lo que describes
suena como dos compromisos reales que van en direcciones distintas... no es que
uno sea malo — es que ambos son reales." The saved profile's
`competing_commitment` field holds whatever surfaces, and the agent's own
caveat — "do not interpret blockers as personal deficiencies — they are
patterns, not character flaws" — is this framework's central ethical stance,
stated directly.
